In 1969, two astronauts walked across the Moon to a robot that had been sitting there for two and a half years, and took pieces of it home.

That robot was Surveyor 3, a NASA lander launched in April 1967 with one big question for the coming Apollo missions: could the Moon's soil hold up a lunar module? Landing was rough. Highly reflective rocks confused its descent radar, the tool that measures height above the ground, and its engine kept firing. It bounced twice before settling in Oceanus Procellarum.

Then it got to work. Commanded from Earth, its scoop dug trenches and tested the ground. It sent back 6,326 pictures. The soil had the consistency of wet sand, and could bear about ten pounds per square inch, strong enough to support a lunar module.

Surveyor 3 ran on sunlight, so when the long lunar night began, it was left in the dark. Its last contact with Earth came on May 4, 1967.

In 1969, Apollo 12 landed nearby, and astronauts Pete Conrad and Alan Bean walked over and removed its camera system and soil scoop. Part of that scoop is on display at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory today.

A robot that couldn't survive one lunar night helped clear the way for humans.
