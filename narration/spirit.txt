Picture a robot dragging a dead wheel across Mars like a plow. That broken wheel was about to make a discovery.

This is Spirit, a six-wheeled NASA rover that landed in Gusev Crater in January 2004, bouncing twenty-eight times inside its airbags before it stopped. Its twin, Opportunity, landed on the other side of the planet. Both were sent to hunt for clues that Mars once had liquid water, and Spirit went to Gusev because it may have been an ancient lake.

Spirit was built to work for ninety Martian days. It worked for more than twenty-five times longer. Along the way, dust devils, small whirlwinds of dust, swept its solar panels clean and lifted its power from sixty to ninety-three percent. Then, in 2007, its dead front wheel dug up bright soil that turned out to be nearly pure silica, the main ingredient of window glass. NASA thinks it likely formed in hot springs or steam vents, strong evidence of a wetter Mars.

In 2009, Spirit got stuck in soft soil. It went silent in March 2010, and controllers sent more than thirteen hundred commands before NASA ended recovery efforts in May 2011.

A machine with a broken wheel gave us some of the best evidence that Mars was once wet.
