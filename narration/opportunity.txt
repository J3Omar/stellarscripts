Picture crossing a marathon finish line on another planet, with no one there to cheer. In March 2015, Opportunity did exactly that, becoming the first human enterprise to travel a marathon distance on another world.

Opportunity was a solar-powered Mars rover, twin of Spirit, that landed in January 2004 on a plain called Meridiani Planum, where minerals already hinted at a wet past. It was built to work for ninety Martian days and drive about eleven hundred yards. It kept going for more than fourteen years.

Along the way, it found tiny round grains nicknamed blueberries, made of hematite, a mineral that usually forms in water. Near its own discarded heat shield, it found a basketball-sized rock that turned out to be a meteorite. And at the rim of Endeavour Crater, it found clay minerals that formed in neutral water, the friendliest conditions for ancient life of anywhere it studied.

In June 2018, a Mars-wide dust storm blocked its sunlight, and Opportunity stopped answering. NASA tried more than a thousand times to reach it, then declared the mission complete in February 2019, with over twenty-eight miles on its odometer.

Built for ninety days, it showed us that parts of Mars may once have been gentle enough for life.
