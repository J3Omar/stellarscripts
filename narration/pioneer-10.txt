In 1972, nobody knew whether a spacecraft could survive crossing the asteroid belt, the wide band of rocky debris between Mars and Jupiter. Pioneer 10 would be the first to try.

Pioneer 10 was a NASA spacecraft launched in March 1972 to explore Jupiter, its moons, and its radiation. Out there, sunlight is too weak for solar panels, so it ran on a nuclear power source instead. It crossed the belt and found far fewer impacts than feared, the first proof a spacecraft could make the trip.

In December 1973, it flew past Jupiter. A radio command took up to ninety-two minutes just to get an answer. Jupiter's intense radiation even triggered false commands. Still, Pioneer 10 returned about five hundred images, including the first close-ups ever taken of the moon Ganymede.

It was designed to work for twenty-one months. It sent its last signal in January 2003, about thirty-one years later, from seven point six billion miles away. Today it is inert, drifting toward the star Aldebaran.

On board is an aluminum plaque showing a man, a woman, the solar system, and its position relative to fourteen pulsars, cosmic objects that flash on a steady schedule. A message for anyone who ever finds it.
