## Quick ID
- Device: Ingenuity Mars Helicopter (NASA's Ingenuity Mars Helicopter)
- Mission: Listed under the Mars 2020: Perseverance mission | Type: Began as a technology demonstration, then an operations demonstration, then an engineering demonstration; the technology demonstration was designed for no more than five test flights in 30 days
- Agency: NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory built the helicopter and managed the project for NASA Headquarters; NASA Ames and NASA Langley provided flight performance analysis and technical help; AeroVironment, Qualcomm, and SolAero provided design help and major components; Lockheed Space built the Mars Helicopter Delivery System
- Launch: July 30, 2020, Cape Canaveral Air Force Station, Florida

- Last contact/activity: last flight (flight 72) Jan. 18, 2024; communications re-established Saturday, Jan. 20 (2024); the Jan. 25, 2024 release says the helicopter remains in contact. Later contact dates are not found in the provided official sources.
- Where left: Jezero Crater, Mars. Exact final location is not found in the provided official sources — Perseverance is too far away to image the helicopter at its final airfield.
- Status: mission ended; the helicopter can no longer fly, but remains upright and in contact with ground controllers (as of the Jan. 25, 2024 release). Status after Jan. 2024 is not found in the provided official sources. Do not call it "dead," "abandoned," or "destroyed."
- Original purpose: demonstrate powered, controlled flight on Mars, and test whether future exploration could include an aerial perspective. It carried no science instruments.

## What it is
Ingenuity is a helicopter — a rotorcraft sent to Mars attached to the underside of the Perseverance rover — and is listed as the first helicopter on another planet. Its rotor tip-to-tip length is about 4 ft (1.2 m), and its mass is 3.97 lb (1.8 kg) (an earlier source rounds this to "4-pound (1.8 kg) rotorcraft"). It stood on four legs on the Martian surface, with a solar array sitting atop the rotors, six lithium-ion batteries, and insulation and heaters for cold nights. It carries a black-and-white navigation camera that points downward and a color camera. Its design used off-the-shelf parts, many from the smartphone industry, tested in deep space for the first time. It was delivered by the Mars Helicopter Delivery System, designed and built by Lockheed Space. It carries a swatch of fabric from the original Wright Flyer. It flew in an atmosphere less than 1 percent as dense as Earth's. Component materials, exact colors, and shape details beyond the above are not found in the provided official sources; a labeled anatomy graphic exists on the supplied source page but its label text could not be retrieved.

## Why it was sent
The goal was to demonstrate the first powered flight on Mars, and the team wanted to learn whether future Mars exploration could include an aerial perspective. It was designed as a technology demonstration with up to five test flights over 30 days. After the demonstration succeeded, it served as an aerial scout for scientists and rover planners, and its flights paved the way for future aerial exploration of Mars and possibly other destinations. The specific scientific problem Ingenuity itself was meant to answer, why Jezero Crater was chosen specifically for the helicopter (as opposed to for the rover mission generally), and any secondary objectives beyond scouting are not found in the provided official sources. For context, the rover team describes Jezero Crater as once holding a lake.

## How it worked
A solar array charged its lithium-ion batteries; the supplied source page states this provided enough energy for one 90-second flight per Martian day, at about 350 W average power during flight. The first flight was autonomous, controlled entirely by onboard systems, and could not be observed from Earth in real time. Its navigation camera autonomously tracked the ground during flight, and deactivated within 3 ft (1 m) of the surface so that takeoff and landing dust would not interfere. Perseverance acted as a communications relay between Ingenuity and Earth — the rover's base station searched for Ingenuity's signal each morning, and the helicopter had to stay within range of the rover. The first liftoff was planned from a 10-by-10-meter (33-by-33-foot) airfield, within a 30-sol (Martian day, about 24.6 hours each) test window. It carried no science instruments — it was a technology demonstration only. Documented challenges: evening temperatures at Jezero can drop to about -130 F (-90 C), which can crack electronics and damage batteries; winter nights left it unable to power its heaters, causing periodic flight computer resets, which led the team to redesign winter operations; dust could block sunlight from reaching the solar panel; and Perseverance's science operations plus the October solar conjunction limited flight scheduling in 2021. Reported operational adaptations include gaining autonomous landing-site selection in rough terrain, recovering from a dead sensor, and cleaning itself after dust storms. Rotor speed, blade count, flight computer details, and exact data rates are not found in the provided official sources.

## What it discovered
Ingenuity carried no science instruments, so nearly all of its results are engineering achievements, with scouting observations listed separately below.
- First powered, controlled flight on another planet: hover to 10 ft (3 m), 30-second hover, 39.1 seconds total (April 19, 2021) — showed an aerial perspective is possible for Mars exploration.
- Survived its first night on the Martian surface on its own (April 3–4, 2021), using its insulation, heaters, and battery energy — confirmed the thermal design and the data was used to tune later nights.
- Energy, telecommunications, and in-flight navigation systems performed beyond expectations during the technology demonstration flights (by April 30, 2021), leading to a new operations demonstration phase.
- First one-way flight to a new landing site: 423 ft (129 m), max altitude 33 ft (10 m), 108 seconds (fifth flight, May 7, 2021) — started scouting-style operations.
- Scouting observation: imaged tracks, the "Raised Ridges" feature, Seitah dunes, sand, and rock from about 33 ft (10 m) on flight 9 (July 5, 2021) — helped plan the rover's route; NASA describes the ridges as possibly carrying underground fluid flow (an interpretation, not a confirmed finding).
- Scouting observation: captured a 3D view of "Raised Ridges" using two frames from its color camera on flight 10 (July 24, 2021) — helps weigh a possible sampling trip; NASA says whether water shaped the feature is still unknown.
- Imaged Perseverance's backshell, parachute, and debris field (parachute width 70.5 ft/21.5 m) on flight 26 — 159 seconds, 26 ft (8 m) altitude, 1,181 ft (360 m) distance, 10 color images (April 19, 2022) — provided engineering data for safer future landings, relevant to Mars Sample Return.
- Longest and fastest flight to that date: 2,310 ft (704 m), 12 mph (5.5 m/s), 161.3 seconds, 33 ft (10 m) altitude, using black-and-white navcam video on flight 25 (April 2022, exact date conflict — see below) — expanded the flight envelope.
- 50th flight: 1,057.09 ft (322.2 m), 145.7 seconds, altitude record of 59 ft (18 m) at that time, near Belva Crater (April 13, 2023) — reached 23 Earth months and 45 flights past its expected lifetime.
- Landings at speeds 25% slower than the original design, on flights 57, 58, and 59 (late 2023) — may allow lighter landing gear in future rotorcraft designs.
- Flight 61 set an altitude record of 78.7 ft (24 m); flight 62 set a speed record of 22.3 mph (10 m/s) — dates not stated on that source page.
- First in-flight characterization of flight dynamics on another planet, using a System Identification ("Sys-ID") frequency sweep on flights 68 and 69 (December 2023) — a reusable method for future Mars rotorcraft; NASA said these dynamic maneuvers had been considered too risky to attempt earlier in the mission.
- Next-generation carbon-fiber blades were ground-tested in a JPL simulator (not flown on Ingenuity itself) up to 3,500 rpm and 0.95 Mach (September 2023) — opens the door to possible bigger Mars helicopters.
- Summary totals reported: 72 flights, 48 airfields, three emergency landings, more than two hours of total flight time.
- Follow-on work named on the supplied source page: "SkyFall" (mid-air deployment concept for next-generation Mars helicopters) and Mach 1 rotor tests; the year for these items is not stated in the retrieved page text.
- An official explanation of any "discovery" of Martian geology, atmosphere, or life attributable to Ingenuity itself is not found in the provided official sources — its results are engineering proof-of-flight and scouting observations, not scientific discoveries about Mars.

## The hardware today
Flight operations have ended, and the helicopter can no longer fly, after one or more rotor blades were damaged during landing on flight 72. That flight, on Jan. 18, 2024, reached 40 ft (12 m), hovered 4.5 seconds, and began descending at 3.3 ft/s (1 m/s); about 3 ft (1 m) above the surface, it lost contact with Perseverance. Communications were re-established on Saturday, Jan. 20; the team found Ingenuity power-positive and sitting upright. As of Jan. 25, 2024, the helicopter remained upright and in contact, and the team planned final tests and to download remaining data from onboard memory. The cause of the communications dropout and the helicopter's exact orientation at touchdown were under investigation at that time. Perseverance is too far away to image the helicopter at its final airfield. Current transmission status, physical condition beyond the known rotor damage, and any 2025–2026 updates are not found in the provided official sources. Do not call Ingenuity "dead," "abandoned," or "destroyed" — the sources do not say that. Its continuing significance: the Sys-ID testing method is described as reusable for future missions.

## Best facts for quick answers
- Its first flight is called a "Wright Brothers moment" on the supplied source page, and the airfield was named Wright Brothers Field.
- It carries a swatch of fabric from the original Wright Flyer.
- The first flight lasted 39.1 seconds and was fully autonomous — Earth could not watch it live.
- Nighttime temperatures at Jezero can reach about -130 F (-90 C), and Ingenuity survived its first night there on its own.
- It was designed to fly at most five times in 30 days; it ended up flying 72 times over almost three years.
- Its design used smartphone-industry parts, tested in deep space for the first time.
- It used 48 different airfields and made three emergency landings over its mission.
- It photographed Perseverance's landing parachute and backshell — at the time, the largest parachute ever deployed on Mars (70.5 ft/21.5 m wide).
- Perseverance's backshell hit the Martian surface at about 78 mph (126 kph).
- Its navigation camera switches off within 3 ft (1 m) of the ground so that kicked-up dust doesn't confuse its tracking.
- Flight 72 ended with contact lost about 3 ft (1 m) above the ground on Jan. 18, 2024; the mission was declared over on Jan. 25, 2024, with the helicopter still upright and in contact.
- Engineers on the Mars Sample Return program asked whether Ingenuity could look at its own landing hardware — which it did on flight 26.

## Terms explained simply (for the chatbot to draw on, not to read verbatim)
- **Sol** — a Martian day, about 24.6 hours — a little longer than an Earth day.
- **Thin atmosphere** — Mars's air is less than 1% as dense as Earth's, so there's very little air for the rotor blades to push against, which is a big part of why flying there is hard.
- **Technology demonstration** — a trial run to see if an idea works, with a small, planned number of attempts (here, up to five flights in 30 days).
- **Relay** — Perseverance acted as a go-between, passing Ingenuity's signals to and from Earth.
- **Autonomous flight** — the helicopter flew itself, using its own onboard computer and sensors, with no one watching live from Earth.
- **Backshell** — the cone-shaped shell that protected Perseverance during its own landing; Ingenuity later photographed it on the ground.
- **Sys-ID (system identification)** — deliberately "wiggling" the flight controls in a controlled way to learn exactly how the aircraft responds — like a scientific test of its own handling.

- Do not claim Ingenuity discovered water, life, or ancient lakes — its own results were engineering and scouting; the "Raised Ridges" notes are NASA interpretations about possibilities, not confirmed discoveries.
- Do not say the helicopter is "dead," "destroyed," "abandoned," or still flying — the sources say flight operations ended and it remained upright and in contact as of Jan. 2024.
- Do not state its exact final location or its status after Jan. 2024 — not found in the provided official sources.
- Do not give a specific rotor speed or flight-computer specification — not found in the provided official sources.
- Do not invent feelings, dialogue, or personal experiences of engineers or of the helicopter itself.
