## What it is
A lander (not a rover or orbiter) with a mass of 2,200 lb (997.9 kg), carrying two scientific instruments: a TV camera and a surface sampler. The surface sampler could reach up to 5 ft (1.5 m) from the lander and dig up to 7 in (18 cm) deep. The TV camera had two lenses (25 mm and 100 mm focal length) mounted under a mirror that could move horizontally and vertically; it took about 20 seconds to transmit one 200-line picture, and could also produce 600-line images using digital transmission. It was solar-powered. A photo's alt text (not the photo itself) describes it as a small metallic spacecraft, slightly taller than a person. Its Centaur upper stage began the journey from Earth parking orbit with a burn; one source notes the stage is "now capable of multiple firings." Power-system details, materials, leg design, and exact dimensions are not found in the provided official sources.

## Why it was sent
The objective was to make a soft landing on the Moon. The key scientific question for Apollo was whether the lunar soil could support a Lunar Module — called the key objective for Apollo planning. Surveyor 3 operated alongside the broader Lunar Orbiter program: Lunar Orbiter 3 was launched in 1967 to find safe landing sites for Surveyor and Apollo missions, and five unmanned Lunar Orbiters together photographed most of the Moon. Why this exact site in Oceanus Procellarum was chosen, and any secondary objectives, are not found in the provided official sources. Its importance: it showed the surface was solid enough for later Apollo landings, and Apollo 12 later recovered parts of it to study exposure effects.

## How it worked
It launched on an Atlas Centaur; the Centaur burn from Earth parking orbit placed the spacecraft on its lunar trajectory. During descent, highly reflective rocks confused the descent radar, so the main engine failed to cut off at about 14 ft (4.3 m) altitude — the lander bounced twice, to about 33 ft (10 m) and then 10 ft (3 m), before settling. Once down, it was commanded from Earth to dig four trenches, perform four bearing tests, and run thirteen impact tests. The first of 6,326 TV pictures was transmitted less than an hour after landing (one source rounds this to "more than 6,000 pictures"). Its key limitation was that it was solar-powered and was left in the dark when the lunar night began. Communications hardware, power storage, and data rates beyond the camera figures are not found in the provided official sources.

## What it discovered
- Lunar soil has a consistency similar to wet sand (scoop/sampler tests, April–May 1967) — showed the surface was solid enough for later Apollo landings.
- Bearing strength about 10 psi (0.7 kg/cm², or 98 kPa), from four bearing tests by the surface sampler — solid enough to support an Apollo Lunar Module.
- Four trenches, four bearing tests, and thirteen impact tests completed by the surface sampler, commanded from Earth, April–May 1967 (no stated result beyond the soil findings).
- 6,326 TV pictures of the surrounding terrain, taken by the TV camera from April 20, 1967 onward.
- Program-level statement: Surveyor 3 was a pioneer of technology that enabled Apollo (as stated in one source's image caption).
- The returned camera system and soil scoop (recovered by Apollo 12 astronauts, Nov. 1969) were studied for the effects of lunar surface exposure — but what scientists actually found in that study is not found in the provided official sources.
- Separately, not a Surveyor 3 result itself: Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter (LRO) imagery of the Apollo 12 site, taken about 45 years after Lunar Orbiter 3, shows the surface under the lander looks darker and the crater area looks brighter than in the earlier Lunar Orbiter 3 image — interpreted as rocket exhaust having disrupted and roughened the soil beneath, with the crater likely containing the exhaust and becoming more reflective.
- Neither source mentions microbes found on the returned parts — do not claim any.

Apollo 12 astronauts removed parts — including the soil scoop and camera system — and returned them to Earth; part of the scoop is on display at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory in California. ## Terms explained simply (for the chatbot to draw on, not to read verbatim)
- **Soft landing** — the mission's objective, touching down gently enough to survive rather than crashing — the fuller technical definition isn't given in the sources.
- **Bearing strength** — how much weight the ground can hold before it gives way, like testing whether sand can support a table leg; Surveyor 3 measured about 10 psi, solid enough for an Apollo Lunar Module.
- **Descent radar** — a tool that measures height above the ground during landing; Surveyor 3's was confused by unusually reflective rocks.
- **Parking orbit** — a waiting orbit around Earth before the main engine burn sends a spacecraft on its way to the Moon — like a waiting lane before the main trip.
- **EVA (extravehicular activity)** — a spacewalk, or in this case a moonwalk, outside the lander; Apollo 12's second EVA included the Surveyor 3 visit.
- **Lunar night** — the Moon's long dark period, when solar panels get no sunlight — this is what left the solar-powered Surveyor 3 without power for good.
- **Lunar Orbiter / LRO** — orbiting cameras that mapped the Moon's surface; Lunar Orbiter 3 (1967) helped choose landing sites, and the Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter (LRO), decades later, re-imaged the Surveyor 3 and Apollo 12 site.
- **Oceanus Procellarum** — Latin for "Ocean of Storms," the large dark plain where Surveyor 3 landed — not an actual ocean.
